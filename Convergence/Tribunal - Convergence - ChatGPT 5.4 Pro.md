According to documents from March 25, 2026, the cleanest read is that ARES did **not** fail. It killed a seductive assumption. In your grounded cybersecurity setting, free-form multi-turn debate was **worse** than single-turn synthesis: single-turn LLM hit **83.3% (15/18)**, while both multi-turn variants landed at **66.7% (12/18)**, with the failure traced to **Architect retreat** and **Skeptic rigidity**, not to missing telemetry or sloppy engineering.

Against **Frédéric Berdoz, Leonardo Rugli, and Roger Wattenhofer’s** paper, the alignment is real, but I would tighten the language. Their study is a **no-stake scalar Byzantine consensus game** with **Qwen3-8B/14B**, and it finds that valid agreement is unreliable even in benign settings, failures are dominated by **liveness loss**, mention of adversaries hurts performance, and larger groups do worse. That is not the same task as ARES, but it is the same **failure family**: individual agents can be competent, while the interaction protocol itself becomes the source of failure. So I would call this **convergent evidence**, not strict external validation. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.01213))

The strongest thing you’ve actually proven is narrower and more valuable than the grand thesis: **current LLMs are better at grounded first-pass judgment than at socially updating each other through open-ended debate**. That is a serious result because you have the things most people don’t: closed-world evidence, ground truth, instrumentation, a working baseline, and a diagnosed mechanism. You did not just say “debate failed.” You said **how** it failed.

Now the knife. There is a material inconsistency in the story that must be fixed before you publish or present this hard. The compendium says **SC-017** was the one genuine win where debate corrected single-turn by moving to **INCONCLUSIVE**, but the per-scenario table in the battle plan shows **SC-017 wrong in all three modes**. That is the kind of fracture a reviewer will attack immediately. Reconcile the narrative, the benchmark table, and the saved run artifacts before using SC-017 as a flagship example.

Your other blind spot is statistical. **Eighteen scenarios** is enough to discover mechanisms, but not enough to be casual about claims like “the complementary strengths are exploitable” or “an ensemble will beat all modes.” Those are hypotheses, not verdicts yet. Right now the docs lean heavily on accuracy. I would add **calibration** and **influence** metrics: Brier score, ECE, abstention quality, round-to-round confidence drift, “who moves whom” asymmetry, and scenario-cluster analysis from the confidence traces. That dataset is a war map, not just a scoreboard.

My direction is two-track. **Production track:** harden single-turn now, because it is already the best-performing path and your own compendium correctly calls it the checkpoint verdict. **Research track:** do **not** spend the next cycle on free-form ensemble theater. Go straight to **claim-level adversarial audit**. Let Architect emit atomic claims tied to facts. Let Skeptic attack specific claims, not the whole verdict. Let Oracle aggregate deterministically over a structured claim graph. That is the shortest path from your diagnosed failure to a principled redesign, and it sits directly between your own “per-claim debate” and “structured argumentation” options.

The publishable paper is not “AI agents cannot agree.” Too broad. The sharper paper is: **when does adversarial multi-agent deliberation improve calibration versus degrade accuracy under grounded evidence?** The ETH Zurich paper itself notes limits around model family and adversarial diversity, which gives you room to contribute a domain-grounded follow-up instead of repeating them. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.01213))

My wild card is this: **debate may be the wrong primitive**. Use **selective deliberation** instead of universal deliberation. Run single-turn first. Only escalate to adversarial review when the case falls into an uncertainty band or when evidence support is thin. If SC-017 survives reconciliation, that is exactly what it suggests: debate helps mostly when the correct answer is **disciplined uncertainty**, not confident classification.

Bottom line: this is strong work. Honest, measured, and actually interesting. The poetry is earned. But the claim must be scoped correctly: **free-form multi-turn LLM debate is unreliable in this grounded cybersecurity setting, for identifiable interaction-dynamic reasons**. Ship single-turn. Rebuild research around claim-localized audit. And do not let the SC-017 contradiction leave the building.
